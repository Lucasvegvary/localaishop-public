Keep a human in the loop

Lucas Vegvary · The Local AI Shop · Napa, California · October 10, 2026

Here's how I think about AI in a small business: it's the fastest apprentice you've ever had. It never gets tired, it works at midnight, and it will tell you something flat wrong with total confidence.

You wouldn't let a first-week apprentice send invoices without looking at them. Same rule here.

"Human in the loop" is just the industry's name for that rule. The AI does the drafting, the sorting, the first pass. A person who knows the work looks at it before it reaches a customer, a bank account or a calendar.

What happens when nobody looks

In 2023, two New York lawyers filed a brief in federal court that cited six cases ChatGPT had made up. None of them existed. The judge fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000, and the story ran everywhere.

The AI didn't fail there. It did what it does: produce something that sounds right. The failure was that nobody checked. Two trained professionals skipped the one step that mattered.

Your version of that is smaller and more common. A text that quotes last year's price. A follow-up email that goes to the wrong Mike. An appointment booked on a Sunday you don't work. None of it ends up in the newspaper. All of it costs you a customer's trust.

Where the human has to stay

You don't need to watch everything. That defeats the point. You need to watch the handful of places where a mistake costs real money or a real relationship. In a trade shop, it's usually these four:

  1. Anything with a price on it. Quotes, estimates, invoices, change orders. AI can draft them from your notes. You check the numbers.
  2. Anything that commits your time. Bookings, arrival windows, "we'll be there tomorrow." AI can suggest. You confirm.
  3. Anything that goes to a customer for the first time. Once you've approved a message ten times and it's been right ten times, you can loosen up. Not before.
  4. Anything about money coming in or going out. Payments, refunds, payroll. The AI doesn't press that button.

The rest, like sorting your inbox, logging a missed call, summarizing a voicemail or pulling yesterday's jobs into a list, can run on its own. You just glance at it.

Make the check fast, or it won't happen

The honest problem with "keep a human in the loop" is that people stop checking when checking is a pain. If reviewing the AI's work takes as long as doing it yourself, you'll quit by week three.

So build the check to be quick:

  • Drafts, not sends. Set things up so AI fills your drafts folder, not your customers' inboxes. You read it, you tap send.
  • One place to look. Everything the AI did lands in one list you check twice a day, not scattered across five apps.
  • The facts it used, right there. A good setup shows you what the AI based its answer on: the price list, the customer's last job, the calendar. You check the source, not just the sentence.

A ten-second look at a drafted quote is the difference between AI that gives you your evenings back and AI that costs you customers.

I work the same way

I hold my own work to this rule. When I put together a written report for a business, AI does the first pass: it sorts the notes, checks current tool prices and runs the math. Then a competent human goes through it, and a separate check looks for mistakes, before it reaches the owner. And you're the last check: nothing goes on the list unless you hear the math and say yes out loud.

It's the responsible way to use these tools. AI lets a small shop get more done than it ever could by hand. You stay the one responsible for what gets done under your name.

The short version

AI drafts. You decide. Put a person on the prices, the promises, the first-time messages and the money. Make the check fast enough that you'll actually keep doing it.
