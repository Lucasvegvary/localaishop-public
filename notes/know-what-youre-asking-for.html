You can't check work you don't understand

Lucas Vegvary · The Local AI Shop · Napa, California · October 10, 2026

Here's a common mistake. An owner tries ChatGPT once, types "write me a marketing email," gets back something that sounds like a car commercial, and decides AI is hype.

The tool wasn't the problem. The instructions were. And a bigger problem hides behind that one: if you don't understand the task you're handing off, you can't tell when the AI gets it wrong.

Here's the honest part. The better you know the job, the more AI can do for you. The less you know it, the more it can hurt you.

Treat it like a new hire, not a magic button

Think about how you'd explain a task to a sharp new office hire on day one. You wouldn't say "do the invoices." You'd say: here's our price list, here's what a finished invoice looks like, homeowners pay in 15 days and the property managers in 30, and anything over $5,000 comes to me first.

AI needs the same thing. The difference between junk and useful is almost always in what you give it:

  • The job. What you want done, in one plain sentence.
  • The background. Who it's for, what they already know, what happened before.
  • An example. A real one from your business. One good past quote teaches it more than a paragraph of description.
  • The limits. What it should never do. "Don't promise a date." "Don't mention a discount."
  • What done looks like. Length, format, tone. "Three sentences, friendly, sounds like me."

A five-minute setup like that, saved and reused, beats a hundred tries at "write me an email."

Learn enough to catch it being wrong

AI doesn't know when it's wrong. It says the wrong thing in the same confident voice it uses for the right thing. The only defense is a person who knows the subject well enough to notice.

That's why I tell owners to start with work they already do well. You know your prices. You know what a fair quote looks like. You know which customers pay late. Hand the AI those jobs first, because you'll catch its mistakes in two seconds.

It gets risky when you hand AI something you don't understand yourself: a tax question, contract language, a permit rule. It'll give you an answer. It'll sound great. And you won't be able to tell if it's right. For those, AI can help you get ready for the conversation with your accountant or the city. It doesn't replace the conversation.

Start with one routine task

Don't start big. Pick one task you do every week by hand, the one you hate, and get that one right.

Maybe it's turning scribbled job notes into a clean invoice. Maybe it's the follow-up text two days after a water heater swap in Browns Valley. Maybe it's sorting the voicemails that came in while you were under a house in Vallejo with no signal.

Get one working. Check it for a couple of weeks. Learn where it slips. Then add the next one. That's how you build something that runs, instead of five half-set-up tools that each need a babysitter.

Know the tool you're paying for

Last thing. Know what you've actually signed up for: what it costs this month, not what it cost when you signed up; what it does with your data; and what happens when you stop paying. If you're paying every month for an app you half-use, now's the time to find out.

You don't have to become a tech person. You just have to be the boss of your tools.

Before you hand it anything

  • Give AI the job, the background, an example, the limits and what done looks like.
  • Start with work you already know cold, so you can catch its mistakes.
  • Don't hand it decisions you couldn't check yourself.
  • One routine task at a time.

The AI is the wrench, not the job. In the hands of someone who knows the engine, a wrench is the best tool in the shop. In anyone else's hands, it strips bolts.
